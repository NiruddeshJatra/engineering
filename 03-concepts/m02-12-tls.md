# 12. TLS: privacy, integrity and proof of identity

*Where we are: the TCP connection to the server is open. Every byte will now arrive in order. But every byte will also pass through your Wi-Fi, your ISP and a dozen routers run by strangers. Before sending your request (and your login cookie), the browser secures the connection.*

## The problem: the path is not yours

Think about who handles your data between your laptop and Bhara's server: anyone in range of your Wi-Fi, your ISP, possibly the ISP's carrier-grade NAT (note 8), several transit networks, and whoever runs the server's data centre. Any of them could, in principle:

1. **Read** it: your password, your session cookie, your messages.
2. **Change** it: insert an advert, alter a payment amount, swap a download.
3. **Pretend to be the server**: answer your connection themselves and collect everything you send.

TCP does nothing about any of this. Its checksum catches accidental damage, but anyone who changes data on purpose can simply recalculate the checksum.

**TLS** (*Transport Layer Security*) gives the connection three properties, one for each threat:

| Threat | TLS property | How, in one line |
|---|---|---|
| Reading | **Confidentiality** | Data is encrypted with keys only the two ends have |
| Changing | **Integrity** | Every record carries a cryptographic tag; any change is detected |
| Impersonation | **Authentication** | The server proves its identity with a certificate |

**HTTPS** is simply HTTP sent through a TLS connection, which runs over TCP, normally on port 443. TLS's older name was SSL; people still say "SSL certificate."

## Where TLS runs (a correction to your summary)

Your summary said: "Browser prepares the HTTP message and hands it over to OS TLS. TLS encrypts the message and hands over to OS TCP."

TLS isn't part of the operating system in the normal case. It's a **library inside the application**: Chrome uses one called BoringSSL, Firefox uses NSS, and servers like Nginx use OpenSSL. The order is:

```
Browser builds the HTTP request
  → the browser's own TLS library encrypts it      (inside the browser process)
    → socket → OS kernel: TCP → IP → network card   (note 4)
```

The operating system's TCP only ever sees encrypted bytes. That's also why TLS doesn't fit neatly into the five layers (note 3): it sits between the application and the transport, and is implemented by the application.

## Two kinds of cryptography, and why both are needed

You only need the idea of each, not the mathematics.

- **Symmetric encryption:** both sides use the same secret key to lock and unlock. It's fast enough to encrypt video in real time. The catch: both sides must already share the key, and you can't just send it, because anyone watching would see it.
- **Public-key cryptography:** each party has a pair of keys, one public and one private. It allows two things symmetric encryption can't do: two strangers can *agree* on a shared secret over a channel everyone can read (key exchange), and someone can *sign* data with their private key so anyone with the public key can check it came from them. It's much slower, so it's used only to get started.

So the plan is: use public-key methods during the handshake to (a) agree on a shared secret and (b) check who the server is, then switch to fast symmetric encryption for the actual data.

## The TLS 1.3 handshake

TLS 1.3 (2018) needs one round trip, on top of TCP's.

1. **ClientHello** (browser → server): the TLS versions and encryption methods the browser supports, a random value, the browser's half of a key exchange, and the name of the site it wants (`bhara.com`). Sending the name matters because one server address often hosts many sites (note 13).
2. **ServerHello and the rest** (server → browser):
   - the server's choice of method and its half of the key exchange. *From these two halves, both sides can now compute the same shared secret, but someone who saw both halves can't.* Everything after this point is encrypted.
   - its **certificate** (below),
   - a **signature** over the handshake so far, made with the server's private key. This proves the server actually *holds* the private key that matches the certificate, not just a copy of the certificate.
   - a **Finished** message, a check value over the whole handshake, proving nobody tampered with it.
3. **Browser's Finished:** the browser checks the certificate and the signature, sends its own Finished, and immediately sends the encrypted HTTP request.

Older TLS 1.2 needed two round trips. TLS 1.3 also allows a returning browser to send some data with its very first message (resumption with "0-RTT" data). That's faster, but an attacker could replay that early data, so it's only safe for requests that are harmless to repeat.

## Certificates: who vouches for whom

How does your browser know that the public key it received belongs to `bhara.com` and not to someone in the middle?

A **certificate** is a small document that says, in effect: *"The public key ABC… belongs to `bhara.com`. Valid from March to June. Signed: Let's Encrypt."* The signer is a **Certificate Authority (CA)**, an organisation whose job is to check that whoever asks for a certificate really controls the domain.

Your browser verifies a chain:

- The site's certificate is signed by an *intermediate* CA's key.
- The intermediate's certificate is signed by a *root* CA's key.
- The root CA's certificate is already stored in your operating system or browser (the **trust store**). It's trusted because it shipped with your device.

The browser also checks that the name in the certificate matches the site you asked for, that the dates are valid, and that the certificate hasn't been revoked. If any check fails, you get a full-page warning.

What a certificate does **not** prove: that the site is honest or safe. A phishing site can get a perfectly valid certificate for its own domain. The padlock means "you're talking privately to whoever controls this domain," and nothing more.

## What your ISP can still see

TLS hides the *contents*: the URL path, cookies, form data, the page. It doesn't hide everything:

- **IP addresses and ports.** Routers need them (note 9).
- **Timing and amounts of data.**
- **Your DNS lookups,** unless you use encrypted DNS (note 10).
- **The site name in the ClientHello,** which is normally sent unencrypted. A newer extension (Encrypted ClientHello) hides it, but it's not yet universal.

So your ISP can usually tell *that* you visited `bhara.com` and roughly how much you downloaded, but not *what* you did there.

## The cost of security: round trips

Before the first byte of an HTTPS request on a new connection, you pay for:

| Step | Round trips |
|---|---|
| DNS lookup (if not cached) | about 1 (to the resolver) |
| TCP handshake | 1 |
| TLS 1.3 handshake | 1 |
| Then the HTTP request and response | 1 |

On a 60 ms round trip to a distant server, that's 180 ms of setup before the first response byte. This is why browsers keep connections open and reuse them, why sites use nearby CDN edges, and why HTTP/3 merges the transport and TLS handshakes into one (note 13).

## Slips to avoid

- **"TLS is done by the OS."** It's a library inside the browser and the server software.
- **"HTTPS hides which site I visit."** It hides the content, not the IP address, usually not the site name, and not unencrypted DNS.
- **"A padlock means the site is safe."** It means the connection is private to the domain's owner.
- **"Everything is encrypted with public-key crypto."** Public-key methods are used to agree on a key and authenticate; bulk data uses fast symmetric encryption.

## Try it

```
curl -v https://bhara.com
```

(`curl` is built into Windows 10 and later.) The lines starting with `*` show the TLS version, the cipher chosen and the certificate's subject, issuer and expiry date. With Git Bash or WSL, `openssl s_client -connect bhara.com:443 -servername bhara.com` prints the full certificate chain. In Chrome, click the icon left of the address → *Connection is secure* → *Certificate is valid* to see the same chain.

## Check yourself

1. Name the three threats TLS defends against, and the property that answers each.
2. Where does TLS run on your laptop, and what does the OS's TCP see?
3. Why does TLS use public-key cryptography in the handshake but symmetric encryption afterwards?
4. What does a certificate bind together, who vouches for it, and why does your browser trust that voucher?
5. What can your ISP still learn about your visit to an HTTPS site?

> [!success]- Answers
> 1. Reading → confidentiality; changing → integrity; impersonation → authentication.
> 2. In a TLS library inside the browser process. The kernel's TCP sees only encrypted bytes.
> 3. Public-key methods let strangers agree on a secret and verify signatures over an open channel but are slow; symmetric encryption is fast but needs a shared key, which the handshake provides.
> 4. A domain name and a public key, for a time period. A Certificate Authority signs it; the browser trusts it through a chain ending at a root CA whose certificate is pre-installed in the trust store. The server's handshake signature proves it holds the matching private key.
> 5. IP addresses, ports, timing and volume; DNS lookups unless encrypted; usually the site name from the ClientHello.
