## What this episode covers
- How computers scaled up hardware to keep pace with complex software
- **The "Tyranny of Numbers":** Early computers used "discreet" components (like vacuum tubes or single transistors) wired together by hand. This was incredibly complex and limited how powerful computers could become.
- **Integrated Circuits (ICs):** The breakthrough was figuring out how to package multiple electronic components into a single unit. Robert Noyce made this practical by using abundant silicon, which led to the birth of silicon valley.
- **Printed Circuit Boards (PCBs):** These mass-manufactured boards replaced tangled wires by having metal connections etched directly into them, allowing ICs to easily connect.
- **Photolithography:** This is the manufacturing process used to make modern chips. It uses focused light, stencils (photomasks), and chemicals to etch microscopic patterns and transistors directly onto silicon wafers.
- **Moore's Law:** In 1965, George Moore noticed a trend: thanks to manufacturing advances, the number of transistors that can fit in a given space doubles roughly every two years.
- **The Microprocessor:** This miniaturization led to the 1971 Intel 4004, the first entire CPU on a single chip. Today's processors pack billions of transistors into a space smaller than a postage stamp.
- **Physical Limits:** We are nearing the end of Moore's law because components are becoming so incredibly small that they face quantum mechanics problems. Specifically, "quantum tunnelling" occurs when transistors are only a few atoms wide, causing electrons to simply jump the gaps and break the circuit's functionality.
